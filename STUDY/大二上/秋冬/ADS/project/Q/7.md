\section{Discussion}

In this section, we provide an in-depth discussion of the relative performance, behavior, and theoretical implications of the four algorithms tested. Beyond the raw numerical results, we examine stability across random initializations, scaling behavior, sensitivity to graph structure, convergence dynamics, and practical usability.

\subsection*{Comparison of Solution Quality}
Across all experiments, the Kernighan--Lin (KL) algorithm consistently produced the best or near-best cuts, particularly on dense graphs. This is expected, because KL explores a significantly larger neighborhood than simple single-vertex flips: each KL round considers a sequence of flips and selects the best prefix, effectively escaping many local minima. As a result, KL was the only algorithm that improved substantially beyond the simple 2-approx local optima in dense graphs.

The $(2+\epsilon)$ big-enough-improvement algorithm performed surprisingly close to the single-flip algorithm in many cases. Because the improvement threshold scales with the current cut value, the algorithm becomes increasingly conservative over time, often stopping earlier than the pure 2-approx algorithm. This leads to faster convergence but slightly weaker solution quality on dense graphs. On sparse graphs, both algorithms behaved almost identically, suggesting that large improvements per flip are rare in such instances.

The greedy constructive algorithm exhibited mixed behavior. On sparse graphs, it frequently produced solutions comparable to or even better than the single-flip local search, despite having no local search component. This suggests that greedy choices align well with sparse topologies, where vertex contributions are less interdependent. However, on dense graphs, the greedy algorithm underperformed compared to KL and even the basic 2-approx local search. This is consistent with its theoretical behavior: in dense graphs, marginal contributions depend heavily on earlier placement choices, and greedy decisions accumulate suboptimality more quickly.

\subsection*{Runtime and Scalability}
In terms of running time, the greedy algorithm was by far the fastest, scaling almost linearly with the number of edges. This makes it especially attractive when quick approximate solutions are required for large instances.

The single-flip and big-enough-improvement algorithms also scaled well, with average running times remaining well below a millisecond even for graphs of 100 vertices. Their cost is dominated by repeated gain evaluations, but incremental updates keep each iteration efficient. Between the two, the $(2+\epsilon)$ algorithm converged slightly faster due to its stricter flip threshold, confirming its theoretical $O((1/\epsilon)\log W)$ bound.

As expected, KL was the slowest. Its $O(n^2)$ per-pass complexity became apparent on dense graphs, where running time grew by more than an order of magnitude compared to the other heuristics. Despite its superior cut quality, this runtime penalty limits its practicality for very large instances.

\subsection*{Stability Across Random Initializations}
One notable observation is that both the greedy algorithm and the big-enough-improvement algorithm showed low variance across runs. Greedy is deterministic for fixed vertex order, and the improvement threshold of the $(2+\epsilon)$ algorithm prevents it from wandering through many small local movements. KL exhibited some variance on denser graphs because different initial partitions may lead the heuristic to different gain sequences and prefix selections.

The single-flip algorithm displayed moderate sensitivity to initialization: depending on the first few flips, it may fall into different local optima. However, the variance in final cut values was small in our experiments, suggesting that many local optima have similar objective values.

\subsection*{Convergence Behavior}
The four algorithms demonstrate distinct convergence patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Greedy} converges instantly (no iterations), but “locks in’’ early decisions.
    \item \textbf{Single Flip} makes many small improvements and tends to plateau gradually.
    \item \textbf{$(2+\epsilon)$ big-enough} converges quickly because only large improvements are allowed; once the cut is moderately good, few such improvements remain.
    \item \textbf{KL} converges in a small number of rounds, but each round is expensive. Its non-monotonic temporary flips enable it to bypass shallow local minima.
\end{itemize}

KL's ability to temporarily decrease the solution value before ascending again is a clear advantage; none of the other algorithms can escape local optima once reached. This explains its strongest solutions on dense graphs.

\subsection*{Effect of Graph Density}
Graph density played a key role in algorithm performance:

\begin{itemize}
    \item On \textbf{sparse graphs}, all algorithms behave similarly because flipping a vertex changes the cut only slightly. Local optima are shallow and plentiful, and the greedy choices are often adequate.
    \item On \textbf{dense graphs}, greedy deteriorates, and local search becomes more powerful. KL shows its largest advantage here, as the richer interaction structure among vertices offers more opportunities for coordinated improvements.
\end{itemize}

This difference underscores the importance of matching algorithmic complexity to graph structure.

\subsection*{Practical Considerations}
From a practical perspective, all four algorithms have distinct advantages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Greedy} is ideal when a very fast but reasonable cut is required.
    \item \textbf{Single Flip} is the best choice when time is limited but solutions must exceed greedy quality.
    \item \textbf{$(2+\epsilon)$ big-enough} provides a tunable balance between time and quality; increasing $\epsilon$ accelerates convergence at the expense of perhaps missing small improvements.
    \item \textbf{KL} is preferred when solution quality is paramount and graph size remains moderate.
\end{itemize}

Overall, the results confirm theoretical expectations while also illustrating clear qualitative differences among the methods.

\section{Introduction}
The Maximum Cut (Max-Cut) problem is a classical NP-hard optimization problem with significant applications in circuit layout optimization, graph clustering, statistical physics models, and machine learning. Given a graph $G=(V,E,w)$, the goal is to divide the vertices into two sets such that the total weight of edges crossing the partition is maximized. Because computing the exact maximum cut is computationally intractable for large graphs, approximation and heuristic algorithms are widely used in practice.

This project focuses on implementing and evaluating three local search algorithms presented in class: the 2-approximation single-flip algorithm, the $(2+\epsilon)$ big-enough-improvement method, and the Kernighan--Lin heuristic, which explores a larger neighborhood based on paired vertex exchanges. We also study a greedy constructive algorithm as a bonus, proving its 2-approximation guarantee and comparing its empirical performance.

Through extensive experiments on graphs of multiple sizes and densities, we examine the trade-offs between solution quality, running time, convergence behavior, and robustness of each method.

\section{Algorithms}
In this section, we present formal descriptions of all algorithms used in this project, including pseudocode written in an academic textual format (for maximum Overleaf compatibility) and asymptotic complexity analysis.

\subsection{Single-Flip 2-Approximation Algorithm}
This algorithm repeatedly flips any vertex whose flip increases the current cut value. It is guaranteed to reach a 2-approximate solution.

\textbf{Pseudocode:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Initialize a random partition $S$ of $V$.
    \item Repeat:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Set a flag $improved = false$.
        \item For each vertex $v$:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Compute the gain of flipping $v$.
            \item If gain $>0$, flip $v$ and set $improved = true$.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
    \item Until $improved = false$.
    \item Return the final partition.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Time Complexity:} Each iteration checks all $n$ vertices with $O(1)$ gain updates, and the cut increases at most $W$ times. Total time: $O(nW)$.

\subsection{Big-Enough-Improvement $(2+\epsilon)$ Algorithm}
This variant performs a flip only if the improvement is at least an $\epsilon$ fraction of the current cut value.

\textbf{Pseudocode:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Initialize a random partition $S$.
    \item Repeat:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Set $improved = false$.
        \item For each vertex $v$:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Compute gain $g(v)$.
            \item If $g(v) \ge \epsilon \, w(S)$, flip $v$ and set $improved = true$.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
    \item Until $improved = false$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Time Complexity:} Each successful flip increases the cut by at least a multiplicative factor $(1+\epsilon)$, so the algorithm performs at most $O((1/\epsilon) \log W)$ flips. With $O(n)$ work per iteration: total time $O((n/\epsilon) \log W)$.

\subsection{Kernighan--Lin Heuristic}
KL explores a much larger neighborhood by temporarily performing a sequence of flips and selecting the best prefix.

\textbf{Pseudocode (simplified textual):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start with an initial partition $S$.
    \item Repeat:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Mark all vertices as unlocked.
        \item For $k = 1$ to $n$:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Among unlocked vertices, choose $v$ with largest gain.
            \item Record the gain and the vertex.
            \item Flip $v$ and lock it.
        \end{itemize}
        \item Compute prefix sums of gains and choose the best prefix length $t$.
        \item Undo flips after step $t$.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Until total gain $\le 0$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Time Complexity:} Gain recomputation costs $O(n)$ per move, and there are $n$ moves per round: $O(n^2)$ per KL pass.

\subsection{Greedy Constructive Algorithm (Bonus)}
This greedy algorithm constructs the cut by sequentially placing vertices into the side that yields the larger marginal cut contribution.

\textbf{Pseudocode:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Initialize sets $A = \emptyset$, $B = \emptyset$.
    \item For each vertex $v$ in a fixed order:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Compute gain if $v$ is placed in $A$.
        \item Compute gain if $v$ is placed in $B$.
        \item Place $v$ into the side with larger gain.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Approximation Ratio Proof Sketch}
We now provide an expanded formal proof that the greedy algorithm achieves a $2$-approximation ratio for Max-Cut.

Let $(A,B)$ denote the cut produced by the greedy algorithm. Let $(A^*,B^*)$ denote an optimal cut. Consider processing vertices one by one. When a vertex $v$ is placed, its contribution to the cut is:
\[
    c(v) = \min \big( w(v,A), \, w(v,B) \big),
\]
because the algorithm places it on the side maximizing cut weight.

For each vertex $v$ and every neighbor $u$ appearing later in the ordering, the greedy placement of $v$ ensures that the edge $(u,v)$ contributes at least half of its weight to the final cut. Summing across all vertex insertions gives:
\[
    w(A,B) = \sum_{v \in V} c(v) \ge \frac{1}{2} \sum_{(u,v)\in E} w(u,v) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{OPT},
\]
where $\text{OPT}$ is the weight of the maximum cut.

Thus the greedy cut is always at least half of optimal, establishing the $2$-approximation ratio:
\[
    w(A,B) \ge \frac{1}{2}\, \text{OPT}.
\]

\textbf{Time Complexity:} All gains can be computed by scanning adjacency lists. Total time is $O(m)$.

    w(A,B) \ge \frac{1}{2}\, \text{OPT}.
\]

\textbf{Time Complexity:} All gains can be computed by scanning adjacency lists. Total time is $O(m)$.

\section{Discussion}

In this section, we provide an in-depth discussion of the relative performance, behavior, and theoretical implications of the four algorithms tested. Beyond the raw numerical results, we examine stability across random initializations, scaling behavior, sensitivity to graph structure, convergence dynamics, and practical usability.

\subsection*{5.1 Comparison of Solution Quality}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Across all experiments, the Kernighan--Lin (KL) algorithm consistently produced the best or near-best cuts, particularly on dense graphs. This is expected, because KL explores a significantly larger neighborhood than simple single-vertex flips: each KL round considers a sequence of flips and selects the best prefix, effectively escaping many local minima. As a result, KL was the only algorithm that improved substantially beyond the simple 2-approx local optima in dense graphs.
    \item The $(2+\epsilon)$ big-enough-improvement algorithm performed surprisingly close to the single-flip algorithm in many cases. Because the improvement threshold scales with the current cut value, the algorithm becomes increasingly conservative over time, often stopping earlier than the pure 2-approx algorithm. This leads to faster convergence but slightly weaker solution quality on dense graphs. On sparse graphs, both algorithms behaved almost identically, suggesting that large improvements per flip are rare in such instances.
    \item The greedy constructive algorithm exhibited mixed behavior. On sparse graphs, it frequently produced solutions comparable to or even better than the single-flip local search, despite having no local search component. This suggests that greedy choices align well with sparse topologies, where vertex contributions are less interdependent. However, on dense graphs, the greedy algorithm underperformed compared to KL and even the basic 2-approx local search. This is consistent with its theoretical behavior: in dense graphs, marginal contributions depend heavily on earlier placement choices, and greedy decisions accumulate suboptimality more quickly.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{5.2 Runtime and Scalability}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In terms of running time, the greedy algorithm was by far the fastest, scaling almost linearly with the number of edges. This makes it especially attractive when quick approximate solutions are required for large instances.
    \item The single-flip and big-enough-improvement algorithms also scaled well, with average running times remaining well below a millisecond even for graphs of 100 vertices. Their cost is dominated by repeated gain evaluations, but incremental updates keep each iteration efficient. Between the two, the $(2+\epsilon)$ algorithm converged slightly faster due to its stricter flip threshold, confirming its theoretical $O((1/\epsilon)\log W)$ bound.
    \item As expected, KL was the slowest. Its $O(n^2)$ per-pass complexity became apparent on dense graphs, where running time grew by more than an order of magnitude compared to the other heuristics. Despite its superior cut quality, this runtime penalty limits its practicality for very large instances.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{5.3 Stability Across Random Initializations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Both the greedy algorithm and the big-enough-improvement algorithm showed low variance across runs. Greedy is deterministic for fixed vertex order, and the improvement threshold of the $(2+\epsilon)$ algorithm prevents it from wandering through many small local movements. KL exhibited some variance on denser graphs because different initial partitions may lead the heuristic to different gain sequences and prefix selections.
    \item The single-flip algorithm displayed moderate sensitivity to initialization: depending on the first few flips, it may fall into different local optima. However, the variance in final cut values was small in our experiments, suggesting that many local optima have similar objective values.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{5.4 Convergence Behavior}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Greedy} converges instantly (no iterations), but “locks in’’ early decisions.
    \item \textbf{Single Flip} makes many small improvements and tends to plateau gradually.
    \item \textbf{$(2+\epsilon)$ big-enough} converges quickly because only large improvements are allowed; once the cut is moderately good, few such improvements remain.
    \item \textbf{KL} converges in a small number of rounds, but each round is expensive. Its non-monotonic temporary flips enable it to bypass shallow local minima.
\end{itemize}

KL's ability to temporarily decrease the solution value before ascending again is a clear advantage; none of the other algorithms can escape local optima once reached. This explains its strongest solutions on dense graphs.

\subsection*{5.5 Effect of Graph Density}
\begin{itemize}
    \item On \textbf{sparse graphs}, all algorithms behave similarly because flipping a vertex changes the cut only slightly. Local optima are shallow and plentiful, and the greedy choices are often adequate.
    \item On \textbf{dense graphs}, greedy deteriorates, and local search becomes more powerful. KL shows its largest advantage here, as the richer interaction structure among vertices offers more opportunities for coordinated improvements.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{5.6 Practical Considerations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Greedy} is ideal when a very fast but reasonable cut is required.
    \item \textbf{Single Flip} is the best choice when time is limited but solutions must exceed greedy quality.
    \item \textbf{$(2+\epsilon)$ big-enough} provides a tunable balance between time and quality; increasing $\epsilon$ accelerates convergence at the expense of perhaps missing small improvements.
    \item \textbf{KL} is preferred when solution quality is paramount and graph size remains moderate.

    Overall, the results confirm theoretical expectations while also illustrating clear qualitative differences among the methods.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This project implemented and evaluated four algorithms for the Max-Cut problem: the 2-approximation local search, the $(2+\epsilon)$ improvement-based variant, the Kernighan--Lin heuristic, and a greedy constructive algorithm. Based on theoretical guarantees and empirical observations, we summarize the main findings below.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local search is an effective baseline.}  
    The 2-approximation single-flip method consistently yields reasonable cut values with very low running time. It performs well on both sparse and dense graphs and serves as a strong practical baseline.

    \item \textbf{The $(2+\epsilon)$ algorithm offers a controllable speed–quality trade-off.}  
    By requiring a sufficiently large improvement before performing a flip, it reduces the number of iterations while maintaining solution quality close to the simple local search, especially on sparse graphs.

    \item \textbf{Kernighan--Lin achieves the highest quality but at higher cost.}  
    KL consistently produced the best cuts across all experiments, confirming its ability to escape shallow local optima. However, its $O(n^2)$ per-pass cost makes it less suitable for larger graphs or time-sensitive scenarios.

    \item \textbf{Greedy is extremely fast but more sensitive to graph density.}  
    The greedy algorithm runs orders of magnitude faster than all other methods and has a proven 2-approximation ratio. It performs competitively on sparse graphs but degrades on dense graphs, where vertex interactions become more complex.

    \item \textbf{Algorithm choice depends on application needs.}  
    For very large graphs or strict time limits, Greedy is recommended. For balanced quality and efficiency, the two local search algorithms are preferable. For the highest-quality solutions and small–medium graph sizes, KL remains the method of choice.
\end{itemize}

Overall, our results demonstrate clear trade-offs among the four methods in terms of solution quality.

\end{itemize}
